The webinar's wrappin' up. You've been on it for like, fifty-five minutes; long enough to feel invested, short enough that quitting now feels like bailing on a movie before the ending. And then the slide appears.

**$997.** Big font. Then the strikethrough, animated, for extra attention. Then: **Today only: $297.**

And something in your chest does a small, flip flop. $297 isn’t cheap. But next to $997, it suddenly feels like you made a narrow escape.

Here's the thing nobody on that webinar is gonna say out loud: $997 was probably never the price.

## What is price anchoring, actually?

**Price anchoring** is the practice of showing you a high number first, so that every number after it feels much smaller. The first number your brain encounters becomes the reference point (the anchor), and your idea of what's expensive gets pushed toward it, even when that number is arbitrary. The crossed-out price is just staging. Its entire job is to make the real price feel like a generous gift.

This isn't some fringe theory. Anchoring is one of the most reliably replicated findings in all of decision research. It's survived large-scale replication projects that took down half of pop psychology. Your brain does this. Mine does too. Knowing about this helps less than you'd hope, which is exactly why it's worth understanding in detail.

## Why does the crossed-out price work on your brain?

Because your brain doesn't analyze prices. It *compares* them.

Ask yourself what a typical 6-week marketing course is worth, in dollars. You really can't. Almost nobody can. There's no shelf of identical courses to look at and check against, no market rate, nothing. So your brain grabs the nearest available number and works from there. The seller knows this, or has been taught this, which is why they make absolutely sure that the nearest available number is one they chose.

Tversky and Kahneman found that even random numbers could influence people’s estimates. Participants watched a wheel land *on a random number*, then answered an unrelated question. Their guesses still drifted toward the number they had just seen. That was random. A sales page isn't.

So when the slide says $997, the question in your head suddenly changes. It stops being *"is this course worth $297?"* - a valid question that requires actual evaluation - and becomes *"is $297 less than $997?"* That one's easy. That feels like math you can do.

You didn't get a discount. You got a different question.

## What's the loss-aversion layer doing?

The anchor sets the trap; urgency is what springs it. "Today only: $297" doesn't just compare those two numbers. It changes the gap between them into something you *have*, briefly, and are about to *lose*.

Decision research consistently finds that people will generally work harder to avoid losing something, than to gain the same thing. The $700 "savings" was never yours. It's the gap between a real price and a fictional one. But when it's framed as expiring, it stops feeling like an offer and starts feeling like that $700 is currently sitting in your hands, with a timer on it.

That little chest-flip during the webinar? That's not excitement about the course. That's the anticipation of losing. It's worth noticing the difference, because they're asking you to make a decision based on the wrong one.

## Why is there always a middle option?

Watch what happens when the pricing table appears. It's almost always three tiers. The $97 "starter" that's obviously the plain-jane one. The $297 "most popular" with the little banner. The $997 "VIP" with all those bonuses you'll never use.

The $997 tier may technically be for sale. But it also has another job: standing there, looking expensive, so the $297 option feels sensible by comparison. Researchers call this the decoy effect. In studies, adding a clearly inferior option can steer people toward the choice it was designed to make look better. (Fair warning, since we do receipts here: the decoy effect replicates well in the lab and gets a little finickier in the wild. But the three-tier table is betting on it either way, and the bet costs the seller nothing.) The VIP tier exists so the middle tier has something expensive to stand next to. It's the same anchor trick except it's wearing a different outfit.

The "most popular" badge, meanwhile, is doing [social proof's job](/blog/social-proof-everyday-life/)... but that's its own case file.

## How do you check whether a discount is real?

One question cuts through almost all of it: **did anyone, ever, actually pay the crossed-out price?**

Sometimes the answer is genuinely yes; real markdowns do exist, and a January coat sale is not a psyop. But in the world of courses, coaching, software deals, and all those "limited-time" digital offers, the answer is usually no. The product launched at $297. It will be $297 next month. The $997 exists only on this particular slide, in this particular font, doing this one particular job.

The FTC calls a never-real former price a *fictitious price comparison*, and it's been prohibited in principle for decades. Enforcement, though, is rare and mostly aimed at lreally big retailers. In the creator economy, the fake anchor operates with all the legal risks of jaywalking on an empty street.

Which means the enforcement is you, my friend.

## The Field Guide

How to read a discount before it reads you:

- **Cover the crossed-out number with your thumb.** Literally. Cover it. Now ask yourself whether the remaining price is worth it for you, for what's being delivered. That's the only question that matters. 
- **Check the price's history.** Search the product name, look at archived versions of the page if you can, scroll the seller's past launch posts. A "today only" price that shows up in every launch is just the price.
- **Treat the highest tier as furniture.** If one option is obviously bad value, it's probably an anchor and not an offer. Evaluate the tier they're steering you towards as if it stood alone.
- **Translate "save $700" into "spend $297."** Savings on money that you were never going to spend in the first place is not savings. The only number leaving your bank is the real one.
- **Notice the timer in your body.** If your chest is doing the flip-flop, that's loss aversion talking, not a judgment. A real decision can survive waiting until tomorrow. A manufactured one is designed not to.

## The Turn

None of this means you're gullible. Anchoring works on negotiation professors. It works on judges giving out sentences; that one's been studied, and the results are not comforting. The tactic is old, cheap, legal-ish, and aimed at all the stuff in your head that predates money itself. Feeling the pull isn't a failure of intelligence. It's proof that the machinery works.

The fix for this isn't becoming the kind of person who feels nothing during webinars. The fix is a thirty-second habit: before you buy anything, read the page the way the seller hopes you won't.
