On every sales page, there's one paragraph the copywriter didn't get to make beautiful. It's usually near the bottom, smaller than the rest, often in a lighter gray, as if it's trying not to be read. The headline above it shouts in friendly bold: *100% Money-Back Guarantee!* And then, underneath, in the part nobody reads aloud in the video, are the conditions. Those conditions are the single most honest sentence on the entire page.

Because the refund policy is the only place the seller is forced to predict, in writing, what they think is going to happen to you. The testimonials are their best case. The countdown is their pressure. The refund policy is their forecast. Learn to read it and you're reading the seller's actual, unguarded opinion of how often this product disappoints the people who buy it.

## What does a money-back guarantee actually tell you?

A **money-back guarantee** is a confidence signal disguised as a courtesy; and the disguise is exactly what you read past it to find. A genuinely confident seller makes refunds easy because they don't expect many: a clear window, your money returned, no questions. A seller who expects regret builds the policy like a moat; conditions, deadlines, "store credit," proof-of-effort requirements; because every obstacle is a refund that won't get requested. The size and shape of that moat is a direct readout of how much regret the seller is quietly bracing for. The louder the guarantee and the longer its fine print, the bigger the gap between what they're promising and what they're planning for.

## Why is a "conditional" refund the biggest red flag?

Because it's engineered to be technically true and practically impossible, and it does it using your own psychology against you.

Watch the classic version, the one that runs on every other online course: *"Full refund if you complete all the modules, do the worksheets, and still don't see results."* It sounds more than fair. It sounds confident. What it actually is, is a filter built on the sunk cost effect; the well-documented pull that past investment exerts on future decisions. By the time you've completed every module and submitted every worksheet to *qualify* for the refund, you've poured in weeks. And weeks of your own effort make the refund feel like an admission that the weeks were wasted, which your brain will do almost anything to avoid. So you don't ask. The condition didn't enable your refund. It spent your time until you no longer wanted one.

The cleaner the logic sounds, the more carefully it was built. "We only refund people who actually tried" is a sentence engineered to make the unrefunded feel like quitters rather than customers. A confident seller doesn't need you to earn your money back. They just give it back, because they're betting you won't ask; and they're right, but for the honest reason: the product worked.

## Why does keeping it feel easier than returning it?

Because the moment something is yours, giving it back registers as a loss; and losses, as you've seen across this whole publication, hurt more than equivalent gains feel good.

Economists have a name for the specific version: the endowment effect. In the famous demonstration, people handed an ordinary coffee mug immediately demanded far more to sell it than other people were willing to pay to buy the same mug. Mere ownership inflated its value. (The effect is real, though later work found it's somewhat smaller than the first studies suggested; so call it a thumb on the scale, not a vice grip.) Now apply it to the thing you bought. The course is *your* course now. The membership is *your* membership. Returning it isn't neutral; it feels like surrendering something you own, even if you've never opened it. Sellers know this, which is why "try it free for 14 days" works so well; not because the trial proves the product, but because by day 14 the product is psychologically *yours*, and cancelling means losing it.

The refund window is short for the same reason. The longer you hold something, the more it becomes yours, and the harder the endowment effect makes it to give back. A 7-day window isn't about administrative tidiness. It's about getting you past the point where returning feels like losing.

## What about subscriptions that won't let you cancel?

This is the refund policy's meaner cousin: the policy that's generous about taking your money and mysteriously broken about stopping. Sign-up is one click. Cancellation is a phone line with a queue, a "retention specialist," a button that's three menus deep and grayed out on mobile.

Regulators have a name for this style of design; the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns report specifically flags obstruction, making an action you're entitled to artificially hard, as a deceptive design practice. The harder a thing is to cancel relative to how easy it was to start, the more deliberate the asymmetry. Here's the part to know, because the legal landscape genuinely shifted recently: the FTC finalized a broad "click to cancel" rule in 2024, but a federal appeals court vacated it in July 2025 on procedural grounds, and as of mid-2026 the agency has restarted the rulemaking from scratch. So that specific rule is not currently in force. What *is* still in force is the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act, which requires online sellers to provide a simple mechanism to stop recurring charges; and the FTC's general authority to treat a deliberately broken cancellation flow as deceptive. A cancellation maze isn't just annoying. It may be unlawful, vacated rule or not.

## How do you read a refund policy before you buy? (Field Guide)

- **Read the conditions before the headline.** The big-font "100% guarantee" is marketing. The small-font "provided that…" is the actual policy. Only the second one is enforceable, and only the second one tells you anything.
- **Count the hoops.** Unconditional and short equals confidence. Every added requirement *complete the course, submit proof, request within 48 hours, store credit only* is the seller pricing in your regret.
- **Find the window, then find why it's that long.** A window shorter than the time it takes to actually evaluate the product is designed to expire before you know whether you were right to doubt.
- **Check the symmetry.** How you cancel should be about as easy as how you signed up. One click in, twelve steps out, is a tell, and increasingly a legal liability for the seller.
- **Watch for "store credit" and "fees."** A refund that returns the seller's currency instead of your money isn't a refund. A "restocking" or "processing" fee on a digital product is a penalty for changing your mind.
- **Screenshot the policy at purchase.** Terms get edited. The version you agreed to is the one that binds them, and the only way to prove what it said is to have kept it.
