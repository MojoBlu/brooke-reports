Somewhere around minute forty, the pitch stops describing the program and starts describing you. *Close your eyes. It's one year from today. You're opening your laptop at the kitchen table... except it's not the same kitchen, is it. You've handed in the notice. You take the call from anywhere.* The voice is warm. The music has gone soft and major-key. And for about ninety seconds, you are not evaluating an offer. You are visiting a life.

Here's the question to carry into the next forty minutes, and the next pitch, and the one after that: who wrote that visualization? Because it wasn't you. It arrived scripted, timed, and placed precisely before the price reveal; and the life you just visited is the actual product on sale.

## What is hope-based selling?

**Hope-based selling** is marketing that sells the imagined after-state instead of the product; your transformed future self, scripted by the seller and priced accordingly. The mechanics are guided visualization, transformation testimonials, and earnings imagery, and the anchor question is always some version of "what would that life be worth to you?" It works because hope is healthy and human, which is exactly what makes it a reliable channel: the pitch doesn't have to install a longing. It only has to find the one you brought.

## Why does "imagine your life a year from now" work so well?

Because imagination is doing the selling, and imagination is a terrible accountant.

Psychologists who study affective forecasting; our predictions about how future events will make us feel; keep finding the same pattern, usually called the impact bias: people overestimate how intense and how lasting the happiness from a future event will be. The imagined promotion glows brighter than the lived one. The visualization exercise is impact bias with a facilitator. You're asked to render the after-life in maximum detail *the notice, the kitchen, the call from anywhere* and the rendering arrives with a feeling attached that the real version, statistically, won't match.

Then comes the move the whole structure exists for: the price is anchored to the rendering. "What would it be worth to never dread a Monday again?" is not a question about the course. The course costs $1,997. The life is priceless. The pitch wants you comparing the price to the life, because against *that* anchor, any number reads as a bargain. (The mechanics of who-picks-the-comparison are covered in [the anchoring piece](/price-anchoring/) this is the same trick with your own dream as the crossed-out price.)

## Why are you so sure you'd be the exception?

The pitch needs one more ingredient, and helpfully, you arrive pre-installed with it.

Decades of research on unrealistic optimism, nearly a thousand published papers since Weinstein's original 1980 studies, show that people rate good outcomes as more likely for themselves than for the average person, and bad outcomes as less likely. Not because we're fools. Mostly because we can see our own effort, our own reasons, our own *this time is different*, and we can't see anyone else's. (Researchers debate how to measure the bias and how big it runs, so hold the magnitude loosely. The direction is not in much dispute.)

Now watch how a sales page collaborates with that wiring. The testimonial wall shows you the exceptions; the student who made six figures, the mom who replaced her salary. The fine print whispers *results not typical*, and your optimism translates it instantly: *not typical, sure, but I'm not typical either.* The seller never has to claim you'll get the exceptional result. They just display it and let your own statistics do the rest. The full anatomy of that wall is in [the testimonial piece](/spot-fake-testimonials/); what matters here is the division of labor. They supply the outlier. You supply the belief that outliers are a category you belong to.

## Did marketers actually write a playbook for hope?

Yes, and it's peer-reviewed. In 2005, two marketing professors published a framework in the *Journal of Marketing* the field's flagship journal, defining hope as a marketable emotional state and identifying, in their words, tactics "designed to stimulate hope." The recipe, per the framework: hope needs a goal that's uncertain but feels possible, and yearning that's been turned up. So the tactics work both dials. Raise the yearning (the visualization, the before-and-after, the life you're missing). Raise the felt possibility (the testimonial who started just like you, the "if I can do it anyone can").

I want to be careful here, because the paper itself isn't villainy it's scholarship, and it explicitly flags the public-policy risks of hope-marketing aimed at vulnerable consumers. But its existence settles a question you may have been too polite to ask: is the hope you feel during a webinar an accident? No. It is a documented, instructable, deliberately produced effect. The warm feeling at minute forty has an engineering literature.

## When does selling hope become illegal?

When the dream gets quantified dishonestly. Hope itself can't be regulated. Earnings claims can, and are.

In October 2021, the FTC sent formal notices to more than 1,100 companies; multi-level marketers, business-opportunity sellers, gig platforms, and, notably, investment and business *coaching* companies; warning that deceptive money-making claims could trigger civil penalties of over $43,000 per violation. The notice spells out what's off-limits: false or misleading earnings claims, claiming no experience is needed when it is, claiming you must act immediately when you needn't, claiming the opportunity is low-risk when it isn't. Read that list twice. It's the webinar's last twenty minutes, itemized by a regulator.

And buried in the FTC's supporting decisions is the sharpest sentence in this whole article: featuring the earnings of an atypically successful participant *implies those earnings are typical* which means showcasing the six-figure student without disclosing the average student's actual results isn't clever marketing. It's the thing the rule exists to catch. The income-claim slide and the "results not typical" footnote are not two unrelated page elements. They are a legal argument having a quiet disagreement with itself.

## How do you evaluate an offer that's selling a dream? (Field Guide)

- **Price the product, not the after-life.** List what's verifiably included *hours of material, access, support, refund terms* and ask if the number is fair for *that*. The dream wasn't theirs to sell.
- **Ask for the median, not the showcase.** "What does the typical student achieve?" is one sentence. A seller who can't or won't answer it has answered it.
- **Notice who wrote your visualization.** If the future you're picturing arrived during the pitch, scripted in the second person, it's copy. Your actual hopes survive being written down later, alone, at your own kitchen table; the current one.
- **Translate "results not typical" literally.** It means: the page's strongest evidence is, by the seller's own legal admission, not what happens to most buyers.
- **Run the FTC's list against the pitch.** Act-now pressure, no-experience-needed promises, risk-free framing, big earnings imagery; each one is a practice regulators have specifically named. Count the hits.
- **Wait out the feeling.** Manufactured hope has a short half-life precisely because it was produced by the pitch. Hope that's still there in seventy-two hours is yours, and it will still be there after you've checked the receipts.

## The Turn

The con that hope-based selling runs on you afterward is worse than the sale: it teaches you to treat your own hope as the defect. You wanted things, you believed in yourself, and it cost you $1,997 so the lesson writes itself, and the lesson is wrong. Hope is not a red flag. Hope is load-bearing; it's how anyone leaves a bad job, starts the thing, gets out. But it is a vector; an open channel through which pitches travel, the way a port stays open for the mail and the malware alike. You don't fix a vector by closing it. You fix it by inspecting what arrives. The rest of this work lives in [Filed Under: Feelings](/filed-under-feelings/), and the wider argument (that being reachable was never the same as being foolish) is in [It's Not Stupid to Fall for This](/why-smart-people-fall-for-marketing/).

Keep the hope. Just make every pitch that travels on it show a passport.
